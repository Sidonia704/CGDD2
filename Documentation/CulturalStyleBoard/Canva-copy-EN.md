## 1. MEANING

- Creative ownership — Players give a ship its identity through their own arrangement of ordinary parts.
- Learning by doing — Players test a design, notice a weakness and change it; each attempt develops understanding.
- Resourcefulness — Players balance armor, weapons, propulsion and energy within a fixed budget.
- Persistence — Players return to saved blueprints after failure and develop a better version.

## 2. PLAY & INTERACTION

### Build → Test → Diagnose → Revise → Fight

Players assemble modular ships on a 3D grid, arrange directional propulsion and choose how much mass, protection and firepower to carry. They test movement and weapons in the range, inspect performance and revise the blueprint before facing arcade combat waves. Combat puts their engineering choices under pressure.

### Freedom with understandable consequences

All construction parts are available from the start. Players may save unfinished or over-budget ideas, but deployment requires a valid connected ship within the mission budget. One core anchors the structure. Blocked exhaust reduces usable thrust; extra armor adds mass; insufficient power reduces system output. These consequences make the player's decisions visible.

### Workshop taboo → game rule

Real-world workshop prohibition: never adjust machinery while it is running. Adaptation: structural editing belongs in the build area; the ship cannot be rebuilt by the player during active combat. Attempts to edit in combat are unavailable, and the player must finish or exit the run before returning to construction. Automatic blueprint-based recovery remains a separate system. This treats a workshop prohibition as the board's taboo; it is not presented as a religious or regional custom.

### Interface as an engineering instrument

The build interface feels like a workbench: a parts catalogue, blueprint identity, cost and component data surround the ship. The flight HUD reads like onboard diagnostics, showing speed, energy and component condition. Feedback should help players explain a failure and decide what to change.

### References — what to borrow

- Robocraft — Borrow the relationship between modular construction and combat performance: the machine a player builds determines how they fight.
- Minecraft — Borrow the creative agency of combining simple modular units into a personal design; translate that principle into a functional spacecraft.

## 3. SPACES

The proposed assembly bay is a personal workshop, with modular frames, component racks and marked work zones. The test range is its repeated practice space: first launch, measurement and return become a maker's ritual. Cold planetary light, scuffed panels and worn orbital structures suggest a working environment shaped by use and maintenance.

## 4. SOUND

An equipment hum and restrained electronic ambience support concentration. Assembly clicks mark deliberate placement. Thruster and weapon sounds make tests readable. A power warning creates a moment of diagnosis; a clean launch after revision marks progress. Exterior sounds use stylized onboard feedback appropriate to arcade play.

## 5. LOOK

Modular blocks, exposed joints and clear silhouettes make construction legible. Graphite and off-white surfaces keep parts readable; cyan marks active tools, amber signals attention and red signals faults. Focused work lights and worn metal textures emphasize practical use and repeated experimentation.

## 6. SOURCES

DIY Maker Culture draws from hands-on workshop and makerspace practice, represented by Maker Faire and student machine shops, with Robocraft and Minecraft as game references for player-built creations.
